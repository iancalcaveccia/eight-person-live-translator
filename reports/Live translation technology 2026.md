# Reliability, Not Speed, Will Make Mesa Top-Notch

Mesa already runs the late-2026 state of the art for a multilingual table. It opens one dedicated streaming speech-to-speech session per speaker per target language, with no turn lock, which is the layout OpenAI's own guide prescribes for group rooms. It runs on Gemini 3.5 Live Translate, the only interpreting endpoint with outside measurements that voices all six Polish, Spanish and English directions. Mesa's 2.6–3.2 s median to first translated audio already matches the best outside measurement of Gemini itself (2.95 s) and the 2–3 s lag of human interpreters, and research shows that waiting longer buys almost no extra quality, so speed is no longer where Mesa wins or loses. What separates good from top-notch now is reliability, room acoustics and the phone. The consistency drop near minute 9 coincides with Gemini's first forced reconnect and then persists through every later fresh session. That points at how sessions are rotated, and at the test method, rather than at an overloaded model memory. Field reports add silent output stalls of 8–51 s that provider dashboards never show. No product or paper handles eight open personal microphones at one table, so Mesa's crosstalk gate is genuinely novel; it needs per-microphone calibration and a rule to mute only on positive evidence. Phones set the hardest limit. Chrome on Android cuts the microphone seconds after it leaves the foreground, and iOS home-screen web apps reportedly lose it when the phone locks. Google's own Translate app gained background operation on Android only in September 2026 and still lacks it on iOS. The roadmap therefore starts with near-free engineering: rotation forensics and a soak-test suite, invisible staggered rotation with a stall watchdog, transcript health checks, and a pocket-mode reality test with a mic-loss alarm. Next it calibrates the crosstalk gate and refines the listening mix. Only later does it spend money on a Polish-capable fallback or relax the no-install rule with an Android shell. Running costs stay near $4–9 an hour for eight people.

## The 2026 template matches Mesa, but Polish narrows the field

In 2026 the large vendors shipped dedicated interpreting endpoints instead of voice assistants. OpenAI released `gpt-realtime-translate` on 7 May ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/openai-gpt-realtime-2-voice-models)), and Google put Gemini 3.5 Live Translate into public preview on 9 June ([Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-live-3-5-translate/)). Gradium launched in late June ([Gradium](https://gradium.ai/blog/launching-gradium-translate)), and DeepL made translated speech generally available on 17 July ([DeepL changelog](https://developers.deepl.com/docs/resources/roadmap-and-release-notes)). Alibaba published Qwen3.8-LiveTranslate on 20 September ([Alibaba Cloud](https://www.alibabacloud.com/blog/qwen3-8-livetranslate-names-the-speaker--carries-the-meaning-_603581)), and ElevenLabs opened an enterprise alpha in October ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/realtime-translation)). In November, Microsoft moved its Live Interpreter to an LLM that "translates speech while people are still talking" ([Azure release notes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/releasenotes)). List prices converged near **$0.03–0.04 per minute**. The leading models translate while the speaker is still talking, which is the property Mesa's 20-second-utterance gate demands.

The architecture question is settled too. OpenAI's guide says sessions scale as "active source speaker tracks x distinct target languages". It recommends a LiveKit worker that translates each speaker once per language and republishes the tracks ([OpenAI guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-translation)), which describes Mesa. Generic voice agents remain the wrong tool. In a test run by a competitor, a prompted general model lagged **11.72 s** on English to Spanish, against 2.65 s for OpenAI's dedicated translator ([LiveLingo](https://www.livelingo.io/guides/gpt-live-translation)).

Polish is what narrows the field. Among dedicated endpoints with published language lists, only Gemini, Palabra, DeepL and Qwen3.8 output Polish speech. OpenAI outputs 13 languages, Azure 9 and Gradium 5, and Polish is not among them ([OpenAI Cookbook](https://github.com/openai/openai-cookbook/blob/main/examples/voice_solutions/realtime_translation_guide.mdx), [Azure language support](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/language-support?tabs=speech-translation), [Gradium](https://gradium.ai/blog/launching-gradium-translate)). **Mesa's OpenAI fallback can therefore rescue Spanish and English listeners but leaves Polish listeners with no fallback at all.** Regional Spanish is thinner still. Gemini and OpenAI expose a single `es` code, and Palabra is the only provider found with Argentine, Colombian and Spain-Spanish output variants ([Gemini docs](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/live-translate), [Palabra docs](https://platform.palabra.ai/docs/speech-to-speech/supported-languages)).

The evidence for choosing among these endpoints is thin and conflicting. No neutral head-to-head exists, and none tests Polish. LiveLingo sells a competing system and discloses selection bias. It measured Gemini's first translated audio at a median of **2,947 ms**, against **711 ms** for OpenAI. But OpenAI fell further behind by the end of utterances (median 3.8 s, drifting to 20.3 s on dense Portuguese) and scored lower on judged comprehension, 4.53 against 4.93 out of 5 ([LiveLingo benchmark](https://www.livelingo.io/research/benchmark-2026)). Gradium's own comparison gives average latencies of 2.9 s for Gemini, 3.0 s for itself and 3.6 s for OpenAI, without defining the metric ([Gradium](https://gradium.ai/blog/launching-gradium-translate)). The main academic evaluation, IWSLT 2026, covers speech-to-text only, excludes closed models and has no Polish or Spanish pairs; systems that chain speech recognition into an LLM won it ([IWSLT 2026 Findings](https://aclanthology.org/2026.iwslt-1.39.pdf)).

The in-person, many-person case is where Mesa leaves the map. Apple's AirPods Live Translation documents two participants ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/guide/airpods/dev9c215ca94)), and Samsung's Interpreter shares one phone between two people ([Samsung](https://www.samsung.com/au/support/mobile-devices/how-to-use-interpreter-setting/)). Microsoft says its Teams Interpreter is unsupported in Teams Rooms meetings with personal devices and works best when one person speaks at a time ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/office/interpreter-in-microsoft-teams-meetings-and-calls-c7efe2bb-535d-42ab-a5c4-d2d91619b46d)). KUDO feeds its AI tier from on-site room microphones ([KUDO](https://kudoway.com/live-events-speech-translation/)), and Timekettle's W4 earbuds rely on a bone-conduction sensor to pick out the wearer ([Timekettle](https://www.timekettle.co/blogs/tips-and-tricks/how-timekettle-w4-achieves-98-translation-accuracy-with-bone-conduction-technology)). **Nobody publishes a design for eight open personal phone microphones around one table.** Mesa's gating, labelling and mixing layers are therefore both its differentiator and its least-proven risk.

## Speed is solved at three seconds; session lifecycle is not

### Waiting past three seconds buys almost nothing

Research and industry have converged on a plateau of two to three seconds, and waiting longer adds almost no quality. At IWSLT 2026 the winning English-to-German system scored XCOMET **0.92 at 2.6 s** of lag and **0.93 at 4.7 s**. The organizers concluded that "higher latency does not always yield proportional translation quality improvements, particularly for the top-performing submissions" ([IWSLT 2026 Findings](https://aclanthology.org/2026.iwslt-1.39.pdf)). Pushing below the plateau breaks things. When ByteDance trained Seed LiveInterpret 2.0 on a latency-only reward, the model learned to produce "trivial translations". BLEURT fell from 56.36 to 48.31, until a quality reward restored it to 55.60 ([Seed LiveInterpret 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17527v3)). Human interpreters sit in the same band, with an average ear-voice span of **2–3 s** and peaks of up to 10 s ([arXiv 2201.02792](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2201.02792)).

The subtlety is that first output and running lag diverge on long speech. Seed produces its first translation 2.3–2.7 s in, yet averages 4.8–5.2 s of lag across long passages ([Seed LiveInterpret 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17527v3)). Kyutai's Hibiki-Zero averages 5.6 s of lag on long-form Spanish to English ([Hibiki-Zero](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11072)), because lag stretches inside long sentences. For Mesa's 20-second utterances, running lag is the number that matters.

Mesa is already at the model's own floor. LiveLingo measured Gemini's first audio at 2,947 ms (p10–p90 2,859–3,104 ms) from a single workstation ([LiveLingo benchmark](https://www.livelingo.io/research/benchmark-2026)). That is essentially Mesa's 2.6–3.2 s, so LiveKit, the worker and the phones add little. The hosted model exposes no latency setting. Google says it internally balances "waiting for context to improve quality and translating immediately to stay in sync" ([Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-live-3-5-translate/)).

The remaining delays in Mesa's own pipeline are small. WebRTC jitter buffers add "tens of ms, not seconds" ([LiveKit community](https://community.livekit.io/t/high-end-to-end-latency-asr-llm-tts-total-1s-but-speech-end-to-reply-takes-3-4s-livekit-agents/1277)), Bluetooth A2DP playback typically adds 150–200 ms ([US patent 11102565](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11102565)), and moving from the 100 ms chunks the translate guide specifies to the 20–40 ms chunks that Google's general Live API advice recommends would save an estimated 30–50 ms ([Vertex AI best practices](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/live-api/best-practices), [Live Translate guide](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/live-translate)). **Realistically, only 0.2–0.5 s is left to win.** Moving Spanish and English listeners to OpenAI, which starts speaking after about 0.7 s, would buy a quicker first word at real costs. Its translations finish later, its judged comprehension is lower, and it bills every streamed minute, silence included.

The delays people actually notice come from slow endings and outright stalls. LiveLingo's median for Gemini, from the end of an utterance to its translation, was 3.1 s, but the worst case was **13.9 s** ([LiveLingo benchmark](https://www.livelingo.io/research/benchmark-2026)). An operator running Gemini in production on the paid tier logged silent output stalls of **8–51 s**, with the connection open and no error. The same operator found that a session created about a second after closing another on the same key took 10–12 s to respond, against 4–5 s when creations were at least 12 s apart ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-5-live-translate-preview-in-production-paid-tier-measured-concurrency-dashboard-409s-vs-real-409s-silent-stalls-and-session-birth-degradation-data-6-questions/180489)). Another developer watched translated audio slow to "a trickle" for one to three minutes, then deliver more than 100 chunks at once ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/live-translate-3-5-high-level-issues/185340)). That is exactly the kind of burst Mesa's backlog trimmer could cut.

Real rooms are also slower than benchmarks: IWSLT's YouTube audio, unlike the talks the systems were tuned on, ran about 1.0 s behind ([IWSLT 2026 Findings](https://aclanthology.org/2026.iwslt-1.39.pdf)). Mesa should keep first-audio time as a floor check, but steer by three other numbers: lag at the end of an utterance, running lag, and seconds of stall per hour. All three can be computed from transcripts that the Live API can timestamp ([Live API reference](https://aistudio.google.com/api-reference/live)).

### The minute-9 cliff points at rotation, not memory

Research agrees that streaming translators break when their memory of the conversation grows without limit, and that working systems cap it. CMU's InfiniSST scored lower on talks longer than ten minutes (COMET 71.6 to 68.0). It also found that its model stops translating unless the standing instruction is kept while older history is dropped ([InfiniSST](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02969v2)). IWSLT 2026 entrants kept only the last 30 s of audio, or only the audio since the last sentence ended ([IWSLT 2026 Findings](https://aclanthology.org/2026.iwslt-1.39.pdf)).

For hosted Gemini, field evidence says the harm comes from carrying state across reconnects. A Google developer removed session resumption from his Live Translate app because it caused "an off-by-one translation cascade where the model would prepend the previous turn's translation to the current one". In one-hour soak tests, **clean sessions passed 98% of the time, against 65% with resumption** ([live-translator](https://github.com/kazunori279/live-translator)). Resumption handles are "not always honoured" ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/live-translate-3-5-high-level-issues/185340)). For a bug on a sibling model, Google staff called disabling context compression "a viable workaround" ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/175234)). Mesa's fresh-session design is correct, and the 98% result shows that an hour of fresh sessions can hold quality.

Fresh sessions have their own failure modes, and they fit Mesa's symptom better than an overloaded memory does. Identical input can produce "fluent, choppy, or near-silent output" depending on the session, and some sessions are "born mute" ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/live-translate-3-5-high-level-issues/185340)). Sessions created close together are slow to respond ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-5-live-translate-preview-in-production-paid-tier-measured-concurrency-dashboard-409s-vs-real-409s-silent-stalls-and-session-birth-degradation-data-6-questions/180489)). And in one study of handoffs after Gemini's disconnect warning (GoAway), 83–197 ms of microphone audio fell into the gap between sessions, while in 3 of 11 handoffs the old session never finished delivering its output ([live-translator](https://github.com/kazunori279/live-translator)).

Mesa's curve stays at 0.97–1.0 similarity for seven minutes, then drops to 0.78–0.83 from about minute 9 onward, with garbled names and duplicated phrases. Three features of that curve deserve attention.

First, minute 9 is when Gemini sends its first GoAway. One operator saw a GoAway with about 50 s remaining at roughly 9:00, and the server closed the connection at 10:00, on 60 of 60 sessions ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-5-live-translate-preview-in-production-paid-tier-measured-concurrency-dashboard-409s-vs-real-409s-silent-stalls-and-session-birth-degradation-data-6-questions/180489)). If the test ran with the five-minute refresh, a continuously read script may never have offered the pause that refresh waits for. The forced GoAway handoff would then have been the first rotation.

Second, an overloaded memory would draw a sawtooth, with quality recovering at every fresh session. Mesa's curve instead stays low through the further rotations that GoAway forces by minute 10. Whatever broke, it survives rotation.

Third, a repeated script flatters the first session. If a session keeps its own earlier output in context, it can copy its previous rendition word for word. The Live API's conversational design suggests it does, though Google does not document this for the translate model. Any new session words things afresh and scores near 0.8 against cycle one, even when it is correct. LiveLingo's drift test ran only 452 s, shorter than the first GoAway, and found all three dedicated systems flat ([LiveLingo](https://www.livelingo.io/guides/gpt-live-translation)). That matches Mesa's clean first seven minutes, and says nothing about the first handoff.

**Duplicated phrases are the fingerprint of a mechanical cause.** During make-before-break, two sessions' output for the same speech may both reach the listener, or buffered audio from just before the cutover may be fed into the new session again. One cheap experiment separates these explanations. Line up the similarity series with logged rotation times, then force the first rotation at minute 3. If the cliff moves to minute 3, rotation is the cause. If fixed reference translations or an LLM judge rate the later cycles as correct, the test method is the cause.

The evidence also points to cheap health checks. Live Translate returns input and output transcripts with language codes ([Live Translate guide](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/live-translate)), which is enough to catch the common failures. Whisper treats text whose gzip compression ratio exceeds **2.4** as a failed repetition loop ([Whisper](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/transcribe.py)); heavy word overlap with the previous segment flags the prepended-turn cascade described above; an output language other than the target flags confusion; and comparing negation counts between source and translation found reversed meaning in **10.2 per 100 sentences in Persian and 4.1 in German**, concentrated in long sentences ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-5-live-translate-preview-systeminstruction-is-accepted-and-silently-ignored-plus-polarity-reversals-on-long-sentences/179912)). These checks must live in Mesa, because provider dashboards logged stalled requests as successful ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-5-live-translate-preview-in-production-paid-tier-measured-concurrency-dashboard-409s-vs-real-409s-silent-stalls-and-session-birth-degradation-data-6-questions/180489)).

Every fresh session also forgets names and tone of address. The only way to carry them over is the system instruction sent when a session starts, and Google contradicts itself on whether that works. Google's Cloud page lists system instructions as unsupported ([Google Cloud docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/gemini/3-5-live-translate)). On 9 November a Google staff member said they are not officially supported, and a user's test instruction was ignored. Yet in the same thread, naming the source language raised the accuracy of Persian input transcripts from about 74% to 99.5% ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-5-live-translate-preview-systeminstruction-is-accepted-and-silently-ignored-plus-polarity-reversals-on-long-sentences/179912)). IWSLT's extra-context track shows what is at stake: supplying context gained one team 2.75 COMET points, mostly on names and terminology ([IWSLT 2026 Findings](https://aclanthology.org/2026.iwslt-1.39.pdf)).

## No product has solved eight open microphones at one table

Mesa's one-session-per-speaker design turns the hardest research problem into a manageable one. When people share distant microphones, the state of the art separates the voices first. The NOTSOFAR-1 winner reached **10.8%** word error by identifying speakers, separating them and then transcribing across several microphones, against 22.2% with one microphone. That was on meetings of four to eight people, with no real-time requirement ([NOTSOFAR-1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17304v2)). When each person wears a close microphone, it does the separating already. The remaining question, for each microphone at each moment, is whether the main voice it hears belongs to its wearer. That is exactly what Personal VAD answers, by labelling each moment as silence, the wearer's speech or someone else's speech ([Personal VAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04284)).

Physics favours the wearer, and the classic cue is loudness rather than waveform correlation. Direct sound falls about 6 dB each time the distance doubles ([Shure via ProSoundWeb](https://www.prosoundweb.com/primer-acoustic-characteristics-of-live-sound-reinforcement/6/)). By our arithmetic, a boom microphone 3 cm from the mouth hears its wearer about 30 dB louder than a neighbour 1 m away; an earbud microphone about 10 cm away gets roughly 20 dB, before room echo erodes both. SRI's meeting research found that comparing loudness across microphones improved speech detection by 18% relative and was more robust than correlation ([Boakye & Stolcke](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2006/boakye06_interspeech.html)). Mesa learned the same lesson when envelope correlation caught no bleed. Mesa's loudness margins are the right approach, but they lack calibration. Automatic gain control and differences between phones and headsets make raw levels incomparable. Each microphone's level should be measured against the wearer's own voice, recorded when they join, and compared over short windows lined up by capture time.

Gating by speaker identity is the research frontier, and the models are small enough to run eight copies in one worker. Google's Personal VAD 2.0 is 1 MB, needs 9.58 MFLOPs and looks no further ahead than the current audio. It cut word error on a conversational test to **27.2% from 41.0%** for its predecessor. Its authors expected a standard VAD to exceed 100%, because it passes other people's speech through as the wearer's ([PVAD 2.0](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.03793)). Google's 2.2 MB VoiceFilter-Lite cut word error on overlapped speech by 25.1% ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/improving-on-device-speech-recognition-with-voicefilter-lite/)). Microsoft Teams shows that voice enrollment works at scale: it builds a voice profile from about 30 s of read speech and keeps it on the device ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/office/voice-isolation-in-microsoft-teams-calls-and-meetings-a9756ea9-4cec-44c4-aefb-6f5d17c89427)).

Two limits apply. No open weights were found for either Google model. And voice identification degrades sharply on short clips: one recent preprint reports error rates rising from 2.4% at 3 s to 18.4% at 1 s ([arXiv 2609.25007](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.25007)). Identity can therefore settle ties on segments of about 1.5 s or longer, but it must never decide alone whether a two-word interjection gets through.

The failure every source warns about is muting the true speaker. Research on personalised speech enhancement names over-suppressing the target speaker as the main practical obstacle ([Eskimez et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.09625)). VoiceFilter-Lite deliberately suppresses harder when it detects overlap ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/improving-on-device-speech-recognition-with-voicefilter-lite/)), and Krisp concedes that its isolation can remove part of the main voice when speech overlaps heavily ([Krisp](https://krisp.ai/blog/voice-isolation-benchmark/)). That collides head-on with Mesa's gate for independently translated interjections. **The rule that follows is to mute only on positive evidence that another microphone owns the sound.** Positive evidence means the same words on both channels, or an identity match. Another microphone merely being louder is not enough, because in a genuine overlap each microphone carries its own wearer. Muted audio should be replaced with silence so the stream's timing survives. Gemini's manual start and end-of-speech signals only work when its automatic speech detection is turned off ([Gemini Live API reference](https://ai.google.dev/api/live)).

Two of Mesa's current cues need a complement or a demotion. Matching the same words on two microphones is necessarily late, because transcripts trail speech by seconds. It can stop the duplication continuing, but not the first seconds of translated bleed. Only an acoustic gate that acts on the audio itself, before it reaches Gemini, can catch those seconds, and it also stops Mesa paying to translate bleed. The language-label cue will misfire on precisely the people at a Polish, Spanish and English family table. Google warns that its language detection struggles with "heavy accents, similar languages" and "rapid switches" ([Live Translate guide](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/live-translate)). A bilingual aunt who switches languages mid-story, or an uncle with a heavy accent, looks like bleed. That cue should only break ties.

Cancelling bleed by subtracting signals is not an option. CTRnet needs synchronised microphones and processes 8 s blocks ([CTRnet](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2405.20402)). Auphonic retired its timing-based Crossgate because it worked only with one person talking at a time, in a clean room, without overlaps ([Podcast Engineering School](https://podcastengineeringschool.com/crossgate-feature-in-auphonic-multitrack/)), and replaced it with an offline tool ([Auphonic](https://auphonic.com/blog/2025/10/)).

Commercial voice isolation, by contrast, already sits inside Mesa's stack. LiveKit Cloud includes Krisp noise cancellation and ai-coustics Quail at no extra cost, and both keep all speech. It charges extra for Krisp VIVA and ai-coustics Voice Focus, which remove competing voices; LiveKit says these work "well when there is a single speaker", as on a personal headset track ([LiveKit docs](https://docs.livekit.io/transport/media/noise-cancellation.md)). In Krisp's own test on 265 real recordings, word error fell from **23.24% to 6.24%** overall. But clean phone calls got slightly worse (3.48% to 3.91%), and one low-bandwidth sample more than doubled, from 9.42% to 21.65% ([Krisp](https://krisp.ai/blog/voice-isolation-benchmark/)). That is a warning for low-quality Bluetooth links. Noise itself is the easier problem. Gemini filters background noise, though not always fully, and overlapping speakers "can cause translation ambiguity" ([Live Translate guide](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/live-translate)). The free noise suppressors mainly protect Mesa's speech detector from clattering plates.

## Listeners need distinct voices, a mixer and calm captions

Every major interpreter in 2026 imitates the speaker's voice from the live audio rather than storing a recorded clone. Gemini preserves "intonation, pacing and pitch" ([Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-live-3-5-translate/)), OpenAI uses "dynamic voice adaptation" ([OpenAI Cookbook](https://github.com/openai/openai-cookbook/blob/main/examples/voice_solutions/realtime_translation_guide.mdx)), and Teams offers "Simulate my voice" with the promise that "Voice samples or biometric data are never stored" ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/topic/c7efe2bb-535d-42ab-a5c4-d2d91619b46d)). Mesa's refusal to clone without consent is therefore mainstream.

The case for keeping each speaker's voice is that it helps listeners follow who is speaking. In an IUI 2026 study, 45 people watched a scripted two-speaker meeting. Mental effort on a nine-point scale was **2.4 when translations used a clone of each speaker's voice**, 3.1–3.3 with generic synthetic speech, and 4.0 for the untranslated original. Participants said the matched voices made it clearer who was speaking ([Ma et al.](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/551675/file/551675_Fulltext.pdf)). Distinct, consistent voices probably deliver most of that benefit even without cloning. Systems now differ far more on voice than on meaning: the COMPASS benchmark found gaps of over 30% between the best and worst systems on naturalness and speaker similarity, but only a few points on translation quality ([COMPASS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03241)).

Gemini's documented voice failures read like a description of a family dinner: voices "might shift after long pauses, assign the wrong gender based on how the speech starts, or get stuck on one voice during rapid multi-speaker conversations" ([Live Translate guide](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/live-translate)). Mesa's per-speaker sessions prevent the third failure, but only while the crosstalk gate keeps each session hearing one person. The wrong-gender failure bites harder in Polish and Spanish, where verbs and adjectives change with the speaker's gender. Pixel's call translation, for example, rendered a woman's speech in masculine Hindi ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/pixel-10-voice-translate-review-3598387/)).

Gemini also offers no voice choice; a field report found that a pinned voice name was accepted and silently ignored ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/field-report-gemini-3-5-live-translate-preview-setup-schema-docs-runtime-mismatch-voice-pinning-silently-ignored-within-session-voice-drift/173369)). **Mesa therefore cannot copy Teams' "automated voice" opt-out on Gemini.** A relative who declines voice imitation can be served only by captions or a separate text-to-speech path.

Disclosure is becoming a legal duty. Google makes developers responsible for consent ([Live Translate guide](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/live-translate)) and watermarks all output with SynthID ([Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-live-3-5-translate/)). The EU AI Act's Article 50 disclosure duties apply from 2 August 2026, with systems already on the market given until 2 December ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2026/07/what-the-eus-new-code-of-practice-means)). Whether a private family app falls under it needs legal review. Either way, a visible "translated voice" badge, which the IUI authors recommend, costs nothing.

Turn-taking is where an interpreter must not behave like an assistant. The dedicated translation endpoints have no notion of turns. Voice-assistant APIs, by contrast, cancel and discard their current output whenever someone interrupts ([Live API capabilities](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/capabilities)). In an interpreter, that would erase the main speaker's translation every time a relative murmurs "tak". Consumer products avoid overlap by asking people to take turns; Microsoft says its interpreter "isn't optimized for meetings with rapid exchanges, interruptions, or overlapping dialogue" ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/topic/c7efe2bb-535d-42ab-a5c4-d2d91619b46d)).

Mesa translates continuously and never locks the floor to one speaker, which is a real differentiator. Two behaviours need deliberate design. Short acknowledgements such as "sí" or "yeah" arrive translated two to three seconds late, on top of the speaker's next sentence, and no study has tested whether to suppress them. Switching languages mid-conversation produces silence by design, because Gemini stays silent on speech already in the listener's language ([Live Translate guide](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/live-translate)). In LiveLingo's test, both speech-to-speech systems went silent when a speaker switched into the target language ([LiveLingo benchmark](https://www.livelingo.io/research/benchmark-2026)). Captions should fill those gaps with the original words.

Mixing original and translated audio follows a settled convention online but is an open problem at a table. Zoom plays the original "at a lower volume" beneath the interpretation, and restores it 8 s after the interpreter stops ([Zoom](https://support.zoom.com/hc/en/article?id=zm_kb&sysparm_article=KB0064768)). Teams gives listeners a balance slider ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/topic/b9fdde0f-1896-48ba-8540-efc99f5f4b2e)). No source recommends a specific volume offset. Mesa lowers the main speaker's translation by roughly 7 dB during interjections; that should become a listener setting, with a delay before full volume returns so it does not pump up and down.

At a table the original voice also arrives through the air, out of software's reach. Apple switches on noise cancellation to lower the other person's voice ([Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/ie/newsroom/2025/11/live-translation-on-airpods-expands-to-the-eu/)). Even so, Which? found hearing the speaker and the translation together distracting in every test ([Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/we-tested-apples-live-translation-feature-does-it-really-work-aClEt6o0zOut)).

The strongest evidence for telling voices apart is spatial. Microsoft Research found that placing translated voices at different positions in space **doubled comprehension** compared with non-spatial audio ([arXiv 2511.09525](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09525v1)). The study was staged, though, without real translation errors or network jitter ([Moonlight review](https://www.themoonlight.io/review/spatial-audio-rendering-for-real-time-speech-translation-in-virtual-meetings)). The catch is Bluetooth. Once a headset's microphone is in use, its hands-free mode carries mono audio at 16 kHz at best ([Audio Precision](https://www.audioprecision.com/blog/how-bluetooth-low-energy-audio-is-reshaping-wireless-audio)). Placing voices by seat therefore helps only listeners whose audio stays stereo. For most Mesa listeners, the cues that remain are each speaker's imitated voice and the caption labels.

Live captions fail by flickering, as earlier words change when later ones arrive. Google's stabilisation cut such rewrites from 2.1 words to 0.1, while translation quality (BLEU) moved only from 20.4 to 20.2, at an unchanged 4.1 s lag ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/stabilizing-live-speech-translation-in-google-translate/)). MeetDot faded text that was still likely to change ([arXiv 2109.09577](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.09577)). Mesa already retracts wrong captions. Showing the still-changing end of a caption faded, and committing only stable text, would make retractions rare. Quietly relabelling a line credited to the wrong speaker beats deleting it.

The draft WCAG 3 accessibility guidelines ask that speakers be "identified understandably" whenever several are speaking ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG3/informative/images-and-media/captions/speakers-identified-in-captions/)). OpenAI recommends showing both the original and translated text, and telling listeners when translation is reconnecting, delayed or unavailable ([OpenAI guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-translation)). The complaints reviewers filed against 2025–2026 products, from garbled names (Apple rendered "British Airways" as "Briti shareway" ([Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/we-tested-apples-live-translation-feature-does-it-really-work-aClEt6o0zOut))) to wrong genders, speech lost when people overlap and distracting double audio, are the same list Mesa's release gates were written against.

## Phones, not models, cap how well pocket mode can work

On iOS, how a user opens Mesa decides whether the microphone survives. WebKit bug threads report that a Safari tab kept recording when backgrounded or locked. But in December 2023, "installed web apps still lose the microphone as soon as they go into background (or screen locked)" ([WebKit 239602](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=239602)). As recently as October 2024, "This is broken on Web Apps that use Add to Home Screen" ([WebKit 226620](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=226620)). A 2025 forum post claims Safari also suspends live audio when the phone locks ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/774239)), so only testing on iOS 26 devices can settle it. Meanwhile, iOS 26 opens every site added to the Home Screen as a web app by default ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2025/06/18/safari-changes-on-ios-26/)). **That makes Mesa's installable home-screen app the most likely way for an iPhone to stop hearing its owner in a pocket.** Pocket mode's black screen and wake lock keep the page in front only until someone presses the side button out of habit. Apple's Safari 18.4 notes list the wake lock as fixed for home-screen apps ([Safari 18.4 release notes](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/safari-release-notes/safari-18_4-release-notes.md)), but whether it is regained after an interruption is untested.

On Android, a web app fares worse. Chrome's microphone stops about 5 s after the browser goes to the background, while "Incoming audio is still played" ([Chromium 374374232](https://issues.chromium.org/issues/374374232)). With battery saver on, audio drops at once and the call disconnects after about a minute ([Chromium 40282141](https://issues.chromium.org/issues/40282141)). Wrapping the site in a Trusted Web Activity changes nothing, because Chrome still renders it ([Chrome docs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/android/trusted-web-activity/overview/)). The supported route is a native app that runs a microphone foreground service, started while the app is on screen ([Android developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/background-work/services/fgs/service-types)). Android 17's stricter background-audio rules now also require such a service to play audio in the background ([Android 17](https://developer.android.com/about/versions/17/changes/bg-audio)).

The market confirms the limit. Google shipped background live translation in its Translate app on Android only in September 2026, and promised iOS "at a later date". It also said more than a third of live-translation sessions run longer than five minutes ([Tech-ish](https://tech-ish.com/2026/09/13/google-translate-adds-iphone-earpiece-listening-and-android-background-mode/)). If Google's own native app only just got there, a web app with nothing to install cannot promise pocket use with the screen off.

The native escape routes are narrower than they look. Android still allows installing apps outside the Play Store. Its new limited-distribution accounts let hobbyists share an app with up to 20 devices, without ID or a fee, ahead of global developer verification in 2027 ([Android developer verification](https://developer.android.com/developer-verification)). A Capacitor wrapper can reuse the Next.js interface, although its option to load a remote site is "not intended for use in production" ([Capacitor docs](https://capacitorjs.com/docs/config)).

iOS is harder on every front. TestFlight's internal testers must be members of the developer team, external testers need Apple's beta review ([Apple TestFlight](https://developer.apple.com/testflight/)), and builds expire after 90 days ([newly.app](https://newly.app/guides/ios-testflight)). Reports conflict on whether a web-view wrapper keeps the microphone in the background even with background audio enabled ([WebKit 226620](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=226620), [Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/727009)). An Apple support engineer calls WKWebView, the web-view component, and CallKit, iOS's calling interface, "architecturally incompatible" ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/767949)). And even a fully native LiveKit React Native app disconnected 45–60 s after the phone locked with background audio enabled ([LiveKit community](https://community.livekit.io/t/react-native-voice-session-terminating-in-background/945)).

Bluetooth explains why translated voices sound like a phone call. Using a classic headset's microphone forces its hands-free mode, which carries 8 kHz or at best 16 kHz audio in both directions ([Applied-EE](https://applied-ee.github.io/embedded/docs/audio-projects/bluetooth-audio/hfp-voice-calls/)). The newer LE Audio standard reaches 32 kHz in and out on Android 13 and later ([Android developers](https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/connectivity/ble-audio/overview)). But iPhones do not support LE Audio for ordinary earbuds ([Jabra](https://www.jabra.com/bluetooth-headsets/jabra-elite-10-gen-2)). Apple's new high-quality AirPods recording "isn't recommended for real-time communication" and is unavailable in the EU ([Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudiosession/categoryoptions-swift.struct/bluetoothhighqualityrecording)). From a browser, Mesa can barely choose the headset microphone, and Chrome on Android sometimes fails to list it at all ([webrtc/samples](https://www.github.com/webrtc/samples/issues/1338)). Browsers hide which Bluetooth codec is in use, but the worker can spot an 8 kHz link from the missing energy above about 4 kHz and ask the wearer to switch headsets.

Battery drain has not been measured anywhere. There is no credible per-hour figure for audio-only WebRTC calls on current phones. Pocket mode keeps the screen on, so it probably drains more than a native session with the screen off would, but Mesa will have to measure that itself.

## An eleven-step roadmap that spends engineering before money

The order follows four rules drawn from the evidence: measure before changing anything, fix the best-documented failure first, then tackle the problem no vendor solves for Mesa, and only after that spend money or relax the no-install rule. The first four items cost almost nothing to run. Together they address the measured drift, the documented stalls and the risk of a silently dead microphone. Effort figures are estimates for one developer who knows the codebase, because no source quantifies them; costs use list prices.

Three details make the first two items work. **First, stagger session starts.** Eight people need 16 Gemini sessions, and sessions that start together all receive GoAway in the same minute, which risks a burst of new sessions like the ones one operator saw slow first responses to 10–12 s. Spacing planned refreshes so that new sessions on one project start at least 12 s apart takes 16 × 12 = 192 s, which fits comfortably inside a nine-minute connection. **Second, open standby sessions early but feed them audio only at handoff**, which keeps their cost near zero, and use GoAway's roughly 50 s warning to find the best available pause rather than wait for a perfect one. **Third, make the five-minute refresh prove itself.** Every refresh starts a new session, and the evidence so far says new sessions, not old ones, are where things go wrong, so the soak suite should compare it against rotating only at GoAway and only when a health check fires.

The suite also guards against upstream changes. The preview model rejected certain setup fields on 20 June and accepted them by 2 July ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/field-report-gemini-3-5-live-translate-preview-setup-schema-docs-runtime-mismatch-voice-pinning-silently-ignored-within-session-voice-drift/173369)). The mic-loss alarm can run on the server. When a participant's audio goes silent or muted although they have not muted themselves, the worker can speak a short prompt into that person's translated audio. On Android, that audio keeps playing even after Chrome cuts the microphone ([Chromium 374374232](https://issues.chromium.org/issues/374374232)).

The "next" items share one dataset that the content-free logs cannot supply: a handful of consented recordings of real family meals, hand-labelled for who spoke when. Only that dataset can measure the two numbers that decide the crosstalk gate: seconds of bleed translated per hour, and the share of real interjections muted. It doubles as the test set for any voice-isolation model. Speaker embeddings, the compact voice signatures used to tell people apart, are voiceprints. Keeping them in worker memory for the session only, the way Teams keeps voice profiles on the device ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/office/voice-isolation-in-microsoft-teams-calls-and-meetings-a9756ea9-4cec-44c4-aefb-6f5d17c89427)), avoids storing them in Postgres at all. The context card is the cheapest experiment on the list and goes straight at the garbled names. It would carry the participant list, each speaker's gender and preferred Spanish variety, and the family's informal tone of address. It is sent when each session starts, and judged in the soak suite before it ships.

The "later" items depend on what earlier steps reveal. A Polish fallback matters because Gemini is a preview model that has already gone through a degradation lasting several days ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-5-live-translate-preview-in-production-paid-tier-measured-concurrency-dashboard-409s-vs-real-409s-silent-stalls-and-session-birth-degradation-data-6-questions/180489)). Palabra fits best on paper, but its default voice cloning must be switched off to respect the consent rule. DeepL's unpublished price sits badly with a hard spending cap. Regional Spanish has no reliable lever on Gemini beyond the context card. Google staff suggest a three-stage pipeline instead: live transcription with a custom vocabulary, then a text model, then streaming speech ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-5-live-translate-preview-systeminstruction-is-accepted-and-silently-ignored-plus-polarity-reversals-on-long-sentences/179912)). That would give explicit control over names, formality and Spanish variety. The price is losing the speaker's voice and adding delay, which only makes sense for a listener who rejects neutral Spanish or declines voice imitation.

Several tempting moves should stay off the roadmap. The 65% soak result and the compression bug rule out session resumption and context compression. Google says temperature is unsupported on its native-audio Live models, where setting it triggered runaway audio ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-1-flash-live-voice-slowly-changing-massive-audio-quality-volume-dropping-on-tts-requests-longer-than-1-minute/142499)). Interruption handling borrowed from voice assistants, generic voice agents, subtracting one microphone's signal from another, and tuning first-audio speed beyond a single test with 40 ms chunks all promise little. Streaming speaker diarization cannot be the main way to label speakers, because NVIDIA's public streaming models handle only four speakers ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/diar_streaming_sortformer_4spk-v2)) and diart runs 0.5–5 s behind ([diart](https://pypi.org/project/diart/0.8.0/)). And the OpenAI fallback should stay disconnected until it is needed, because OpenAI bills every streamed minute, silence included; by our arithmetic, for a table of three Spanish, three Polish and two English speakers, the eleven sessions OpenAI could cover would cost about $22 an hour if kept connected.

Running costs otherwise stay modest. For the same table, at Gemini's list prices and assuming someone is always talking, translation costs roughly $4.40 an hour when audio is sent only during speech. It costs $8.90 if every session streamed continuously. The crosstalk gate lowers both by not sending bleed. One prerequisite is non-negotiable: Google uses free-tier data to improve its products, so any key on the free tier sends family audio to Google for that purpose ([Gemini pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)).

## Conclusion

Taken together, the evidence moves Mesa's competitive edge from the model to the system wrapped around it. Every serious endpoint now lands near three seconds and a few cents a minute. What a family feels depends on how sessions are started and retired, which microphone owns which sound, and what a phone does in a pocket. These problems yield to engineering rather than spending, and several fixes lower cost while raising quality, such as gating bleed before it reaches Gemini. The corollary is that Mesa's measurement has to mature before its features do. A consistency score that a model can game by remembering its own output cannot tell a real regression from a session that merely words things differently. A preview model whose behaviour shifts under developers needs a standing soak suite as much as it needs code fixes.

The deeper tension lies between two of the owner's promises. Per-speaker sessions turned overlap separation, which research still solves mainly offline, into a decision about which microphone owns a sound. That makes the crosstalk gate both Mesa's moat and the one place where independent interjections and correct speaker labels can fail together, so it deserves more testing per line of code than anything else in the system. And "no code to install" cannot coexist with dependable pocket use with the screen off on Android, while on iOS even Google has yet to ship background translation in its own app. Sooner or later the family will choose between a screen-on pocket mode that is good and a sideloaded Android wrapper that is reliable. The microphone-uptime and battery numbers from the pocket-mode test will turn that choice from a guess into a decision.
